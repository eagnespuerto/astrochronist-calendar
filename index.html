    Astrochronism

    Astrochronism is the idea of basing time on astronomical things and events instead of arbitrary Earth ones, especially those within our solar system, our stellar neighbourhood, and our galaxy.

    Most of the calendar we live by is inherited custom. Month lengths come from Roman politics, the year count from a medieval estimate of a birth date, time zones from railway schedules, and leap seconds from committee decisions. None of it is wrong, but very little of it is about the sky.

    An astrochronist calendar counts from things anyone can measure anywhere: a planet crossing its equinox, the Moon returning to new, the light-travel geometry between the Sun's barycentre and the centre of the Milky Way. Its day numbers mean the same thing to every observer, and they keep working once people live somewhere other than Earth.

    The systems on this site

    Gregorian, local and UTC
    The civil calendar, kept here as the reference point everything converts to and from.
    Martian
    Mars Years numbered from the northern spring equinox of 1955 (MY 1), each split into twelve months of 30° of solar longitude, with sols counted from Airy-0 midnight. Months near aphelion run longer because Mars moves slowest there.
    Lunar Standard Time
    Kenneth L. Lee's 1970 clock for the Moon, which starts at the moment Neil Armstrong stepped onto the surface. A lunar day is one synodic month, divided into 30 cycles; twelve lunar days make a lunar year.
    BJD · Sol–Sgr A*
    Whole days of Barycentric Julian Date measured toward Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the galactic centre. Days are grouped in hectodays (100) and kilodays (1,000).
    BJD · Sol–α Cen
    The same count measured toward Alpha Centauri A, the nearest star system. The two BJD calendars differ by a few minutes at most, depending on where Earth is in its orbit.

    Accuracy

    Everything is computed in your browser with compact formulas: the Mars24 / Allison and McEwen (2000) model for Mars, a mean synodic month for the Moon, and a low-precision Earth orbit (plus Jupiter and Saturn's pull on the Sun) for barycentric corrections, good to about two seconds. TT − UTC is held at 69.184 s, so dates before 1972 can be off by up to a minute. For publication-grade timing, use astropy or JPL Horizons.
